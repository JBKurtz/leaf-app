How it works

Your camera gets you close. Your eyes finish the job.

A photo can tell an oak from a pine. It often can't tell one oak from the next. Leaf matches your photos to a species when it can and to a genus when it can't, then guides your own observations to land the identification, using only the plants that grow where you're standing.

1

Point the camera

Frame a plant in the leaf and tap Identify. Leaf takes up to three photos, from the whole plant to a close-up, zooming in on its own, and reads them as you point.

It matches the photos to the plants that grow here: to a species when it's sure, otherwise to a genus.

2

Guided observations

When the photos match a genus, Leaf says so and how many of its species grow nearby. Here it's a eucalyptus, with 21 kinds in the watershed; guided observations land the species.

Leaf guides you to look at one thing at a time, always the one that splits the remaining plants best. Are the leaves wedge-shaped at the base? Is the fruit dry? Each answer drops the plants that don't fit.

Every question comes with a drawing, and you can answer "Can't tell". The count of plants that still fit goes down as you go.

3

Make the call

Open a candidate and check it against your plant: its size and habit, its leaves, its fruit, side by side with photos and its look-alikes.

You decide. "This is the plant" saves it to your field log with the place and date; "Not this one" moves you to the next candidate.

4

Meet the plant

Every identified plant opens into its profile, page after page a swipe apart, from what sets it apart to who has eaten it and how to grow it. The pages are below.

The profile

Identify a plant, then learn all there is to know.

Over 12,500 California plants have a full profile today. Here are the pages of one of them, the Coast Live Oak.

01 · Overview

The plant at a glance

Photos, its names, whether it's native, and badges for edible and medicinal plants. A short description, quick facts such as height and lifespan, and what sets it apart from its relatives.

02 · Identify

Telling it from its look-alikes

The plants it's most often confused with, especially the ones growing near you, compared trait by trait: habit, leaf shape, margin, fruit timing, bark.

03 · Form

Every part of the plant

Leaves, flowers, fruit and seed, stem and bark, one at a time, with every recorded trait grouped by what you'd look at: shape, edge, surface, size. Hand-lens details sit behind a setting.

04 · The year

Its seasons

When it flowers and when it fruits, month by month, with today's month marked so you know what to look for now.

05 · Range and habitat

Where it grows wild

A map of every watershed it's been recorded in, the elevations it grows at, the habitats it favors, its climate, and its native range.

06 · Uses

Materials and traditions

Its uses beyond food and medicine, from firewood and timber to ceremony and trade, with each record naming the people whose knowledge it is.

07 · Edible and medicinal

Food and medicine, and who used them

How the plant has been prepared, step by step, with each record naming the people who used it. Traditional uses are recorded as history, never as a recommendation.

08 · Safety

Before you touch it or eat it

Whether it's toxic and how seriously, for people and for pets and livestock, which parts, and the signs of poisoning to recognize. Shown here on Four O'clock.

09 · Cultivate and grow

If you plant one

Water, light, soil and pH, drought and shade tolerance, maintenance, and how it's propagated, with an overview of how it's grown.

10 · Related

Its family, and its neighbors

The family line from family to variety, the relatives that grow near you, its other names, and the plants it grows beside.

Photos

Photos and drawings

A dozen or more photos of many plants, with botanical drawings, every one credited to its photographer and licence.

Place

It starts where you stand.

Leaf knows your elevation and watershed, the park you're in, and the habitats and plant communities around you, and it lists the plants recorded nearby, with filters for natives, flowering plants, and plants that are edible or medicinal. Identification only considers plants that grow where you are. Discover how this land's first people found use of the plants found here.

Sources

Where the information comes from.

Every trait and fact in Leaf traces to an authoritative, peer-reviewed flora, such as the Flora of North America. Software chooses which question to ask next; the botany itself comes from the sources.
